PART 1 - What is an NFT?

NFT stands for non-fungible token. Lets see what we can glean from that

  • - Fungible is a very specific term in economics[3]. A thing is fungible if different versions of it are functionally the same. Money is fungible, because another dollar spends just as well as any other. Maybe some artifacts or special misprints are more or less valuable, but on the whole all dollars are worth $1.00 exactly. All unopened pringles cans can be exchanged for another one, and you'll pay the same price for it at Walmart. So, a non-fungible token would be a token that is unique.

  • - The term NFT is used often in computer contexts, here when we say token we mean a piece of computer code. Tokens are super important and make a lot of the web work together. Your browser has a collection of smaller tokens called cookies, that let websites remember who you are between visits. The auto log in system of your email, the shows on your watchlist, the progress of your online training module, and the two factor authentication systems many workplaces use are all tokens. So, a non-fungible token would be a unique piece of code that doesn't exist anywhere else.

Which sounds wonderful! The concept being implemented here is "strict uniqueness". All packs of notecards you buy from the store are uniquely a stack of notecards. They aren't chips, they aren't TV screens, they have a unique form and unique function. But *this* stack of notecards I have in my hand is the only stack of notecards with this doodle of a half bunny half dog thing I drew on it.

There are no other stack of notecards that have the strictly unique property of being *this* stack of notecards. A strictly unique piece of code would be cool for security and cryptographic programming, it could bring all the security of a front door key directly to all computational efforts.
Unfortunately, that's not what an NFT is. For one, the thing we’re describing is impossible, code is just math, and you can’t own a piece of math[4]. Over the years that NFTs have existed, many writers have done an excellent job explaining what NFTs actually are, but the short version is this: A NFT is a claim of ownership on a blockchain ledger that can be bought, sold, and transferred. What exactly you’re owning is ethereal.
    It works the same as a stock[5], I “own 1 stock of apple” just means that the stock market exchange agrees that I have the right to 1 small part of the apple company.

  • - The people at Apple don't work for me

  • - I don't have any ownership over Apple's building, or any of their products

  • - However, when Apple succeeds it's good for me, the demand for my stock goes up, so the value of the stock goes up.[6]

The NFT is the same, a blockchain ledger records that I own a small "smart contract".[7]

  • - The creator of the NFT/smart contract does not work for me

  • - I have no ownership over any of the code (again, you can’t own a piece of math)

  • - I can’t hold or change the smart contract, I often can’t see code making up the smart contract However, there is a NFT market system that knows that I “own the NFT” and when people decide they want it, the value of the NFT goes up.

Usually the “smart contract” part includes a photo, but it doesn’t necessarily have to. Some NFT projects use them as game pieces, game currency, game characters etc. etc. The main limit is size, a smart contract has to be super small, about the size of a photo taken by a nice camera, so that's the most common usage.


I want to underline this point in particular: If anyone ever claims that you’re buying a piece of digital art when you buy an NFT, they are lying.[8] You are buying the right to claim ownership of that art, but the legitimacy of that claim is purely founded on the blockchain used to prescribe the ownership. Immediately we see the issues start to rise up, unlike stocks the same NFT can be traded on more than one blockchain[9]

  • - Any other blockchains will not acknowledge your purchase

  • - Anyone who doesn’t know what blockchain you're using will have no way to verify your purchase

  • - Anyone who specifically doesn’t like the blockchain you use will definitely not respect your ownership claim.

  • - Anyone at any time can take the photo you “own”, download it, and mint it on a different or even on the same blockchain, creating a second copy.

The NFT you have is unique, but not strictly unique, anyone can make a copy at any time, and theres nothing you can do to stop it.

So, NFTs are not exactly the cool art ownership tool they claim to be, but they are still an interesting system that have some stock-like properties, maybe there is something we can use it for.   3. The banks realized that they made more money on the bond per home buyer than they made on the mortgage that home buyer was paying, so they made it really easy to buy a home and really easy to get a business loan to build a home. The result was more supply and more demand, so the bank made more money, but speculative home buyers decided to snap up a lot of the homes on the market.
  4. The result was counter-intuitive, more supply should be driving competition up and the price down, but instead more supply encouraged more speculative buying, which drove the price up which encouraged more speculative buying, which drove the price up which encouraged more speculative buying. The whole thing was a bubble, and when the teaser rates[15] on these homes expired
  5. The bubble popped. Both legitimate and speculative buyers defaulted on their loans, so the mortgages were worthless, so the bonds failed, so all the people who invested in the super safe bonds lost all their money, and the whole thing cascaded into economic free fall.

Bitcoin is a large decentralized read-only ledger.
We can imagine a much simpler implementation between friends. Consider my roommates from college: Me, Nick, Ian, and Olivia go shopping for groceries every weekend, we get food and pay for each other, and often make 5 and 10 dollar bets. It gets cumbersome and kinda silly to exchange money over and over again, so we put together an IOU ledger.[20]The system is simple, I buy Ian taco bell, and just jot down how much he owes me. Nick covers the grocery run for everyone, and adds up how much we all owe him and jots it down. Olivia bets on and loses mario party (again)[21], and owes Nick 5$, so she jots it down. At the end of month (usually when rent comes around), we check everyone's totals and square up with Venmo or cash. This saves a lot of hassle, instead of paying Ian 5$, then Ian paying me 5$, then me paying Ian 5$, then Ian paying me 5$, we just see that we’re even, and move on.
Obviously this only worked because all 4 of us trust each other, and could trust the IOU on the shared note document was “as good as real money”. If we want to make this system work with strangers, we need to add a few features. We already know[22] how to implement a signature system, which can be used to ensure that all the transactions are legitimate.[23] This technology has been used for unpopular digital currencies for decades, and is the same technology that lets you send emails. The difference is that bitcoin implements a decentralized ledger, everyone has a copy of the “shared note document” and all the copies are kept in sync.
The syncing system is pretty simple, when I want to give you a bitcoin I just announce to all other users that I’m giving you a bitcoin and everyone writes it down. We all agree to only write down a transaction if it's correctly signed, and never write down a transaction that would send someone’s balance into the negatives. So how do we make sure that everyone is writing down all the transactions correctly? It's easy to imagine that I simply choose to erase some of my friends' announcements and insist to everyone else that they have more bitcoin than they really do, or that some glitch causes me to miss a transaction or screw up copying someone’s signature. Bitcoin implements a system called proof of work: Given a block of pending transactions, calculate[24] the extra block signature that would be needed to make a hash function output zero.[25]This process is designed to be hard to work out, but once someone suggests an answer it's super easy to verify it's correct with your own copy of the ledger. As a reward for their calculation everyone agrees to add a free “solver gets 1 free bitcoin from the ether” transaction. Then, everyone agrees to only use the version of the chain with the longest history. This system would seem to work well, the hash outputs keep everyone in sync, and if I wanted to create a fake block, I’d be able to set one up, but then I’d have to calculate the next one, and the next one, and the next one, doing all the calculations alone since my fake block has poisoned all the future blocks. The result is a race, I have to calculate faster than everyone else to keep my lie up, if one real chain user beats me, their chain of longer history supersedes mine and I lose my fake blocks.
But when implemented in the real world, we hit issues! It's not impossible for a bad actor to keep up their lie with enough computing power, so the hash function needs to scale with the computing power being used. This created diminishing returns on upgrading your computer, but diminishing returns are still returns, so an arms race started and warehouses of server racks have been plucking away at calculations ever since, causing gigantic energy draws and tons of waste. Additionally, because it's basically impossible to undo a transaction after two or three blocks get stacked on top of it, anytime someone gets scammed its permanent and unfixable… unless you have a bunch of money
